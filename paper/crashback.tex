\section{Motivation}

After a large single-day decline, some stocks rebound within weeks while others
keep falling. We ask a single question: at the moment a stock crashes, using only
information available at that time, can we predict \emph{which} stocks recover, by
how much, and how quickly? A crash is defined as a daily adjusted return of
$-10\%$ or worse, and every qualifying day is treated as its own prediction event
(no cooldown or future-dependent suppression), which reproduces the information set
a forecaster would actually face in real time.

The underlying hypothesis is that a crash reflects one of two very different things:
temporary, overreaction-driven price damage, or genuine long-term fundamental
damage. If the two are separable from contemporaneous information, then recovery
should be predictable above its base rate. We therefore frame the problem as
\emph{calibrated probability prediction} rather than as a trading system: the
priority is a defensible, leakage-free historical dataset, honest base rates, and a
measurement of whether successive information sources---price behavior, market and
sector context, point-in-time fundamentals, and later contemporaneous
filings---add predictive value. Concretely, the primary target is the probability
that a stock's close reaches at least $+10\%$ above its crash-day close at some
point within the next $20$ trading days.

\section{Data}

All market data come from Wharton Research Data Services (WRDS): CRSP for
securities, prices, and delistings, and Compustat for fundamentals. To avoid
survivorship bias, the universe is built from the full historical cross-section of
US-listed common stock---including securities that were later delisted, acquired,
renamed, or went bankrupt---rather than from today's survivors. We restrict to
common shares on NYSE, NYSE American, and Nasdaq and exclude ETFs, funds, ADRs,
warrants, and preferred shares.

We use the CRSP CIZ tables: daily prices (\texttt{dsf\_v2}), the historical security
master with full ticker history (\texttt{stocknames\_v2}), and delisting records
(\texttt{dsedelist}). Crash detection uses total returns (split- and
dividend-adjusted) so that corporate actions cannot manufacture false crashes, and
delisting returns are preserved so that terminal outcomes (e.g.\ bankruptcies) are
recorded rather than silently dropped. Fundamentals come from Compustat's unrestated
quarterly data (\texttt{comp\_urq}), dated by \emph{public availability} (the report
date, \texttt{rdq}) rather than by fiscal period end, so a quarter's figures only
become a feature after they were actually released. CRSP and Compustat are linked
through \texttt{ccmxpf\_lnkhist} (\texttt{permno}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{gvkey}). For
the V2 extension, contemporaneous SEC filings are retrieved from EDGAR and keyed by
each filing's exact acceptance timestamp, so that only documents public by the
crash-day close are admissible.

Every feature is required to be knowable by the prediction timestamp (the crash-day
close), and provider-specific fields are normalized into canonical schemas as early
as possible so that downstream stages depend only on the schema, not on the vendor.
Table~\ref{tab:data} summarizes the resulting dataset.

\section{Model 0: The Base Rate}
\label{sec:m0}

Before using any features, we establish the unconditional benchmark: ignore
everything about the crash and predict the same recovery probability for every event.
This is Model 0, and it is the bar that any feature-based model must clear. For this
baseline we adopt the plainest notion of recovery---\emph{earning money one year
later}---defined as a positive total holding return over the $252$ trading days
following the crash-day close.

The one-year return is computed survivorship-safely by compounding total daily
returns over that window, so stock splits, dividends, and delisting or bankruptcy
terminal returns are all reflected in the outcome; a crashed stock that is delisted
within the year contributes its realized (often near-total) loss rather than
disappearing from the sample. Crashes occurring within one trading year of the data
edge cannot be observed for a full year and are censored rather than labeled. This
leaves $262{,}906$ observed one-year outcomes in the liquid study pool.

The distribution of these one-year returns is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:m0}. After a
crash, an investor earns money one year later only $46.9\%$ of the time---less than a
coin flip. The distribution is strongly right-skewed: the \emph{mean} one-year return
is positive ($+10.2\%$), but the \emph{median} is negative ($-4.6\%$), and more than
one crash in five loses at least half its value. In other words, ``buy the dip'' at
the one-year horizon is, on average, a losing proposition for the typical stock; the
positive mean is driven by a minority of large recoverers in the right tail, not by
the median outcome. This is precisely the gap that a predictive model must
exploit---separating that recovering minority from the majority that continues to
decline.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/m0_return_hist.pdf}
\caption{Distribution of one-year total returns after a crash, over $262{,}906$
observed events in the liquid study pool (survivorship-safe: splits, dividends, and
delisting/bankruptcy terminal returns included; crashes within a year of the data
edge censored). The median return (dashed, red) falls below break-even while the mean
(dashed, green) is positive, reflecting the right skew. Returns above $+200\%$
($2.9\%$ of events, up to $+3{,}800\%$) are collected in the final bin.}
\label{fig:m0}
\end{figure}

The picture is strongly horizon-dependent. At a shorter $60$-trading-day
(roughly three-month) window (Figure~\ref{fig:m0-60d}), the outcome is closer to a
coin flip: $49.7\%$ of crashes earn money, with a median essentially at break-even
and a mean of only $+3\%$. Extending the horizon to one year does not improve the
\emph{typical} outcome---it worsens it. As the holding period lengthens the
distribution fans out: the right tail stretches (winners compound, lifting the mean
to $+10.2\%$) while the bulk drifts left (the median falls from $0\%$ to $-4.6\%$). A
crash thus offers a near-even short-term gamble that, for the median stock,
deteriorates over the following year, even as a widening minority of large recoverers
pulls the average upward.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/m0_return_hist_60d.pdf}
\caption{Distribution of $60$-trading-day total returns after a crash ($267{,}113$
observed events; same survivorship-safe construction as Figure~\ref{fig:m0}). The
distribution is more symmetric and centered near break-even, with the median and mean
close to zero. Returns above $+100\%$ are collected in the final bin.}
\label{fig:m0-60d}
\end{figure}

Finally, the base rate is regime-dependent, which cautions against reading any single
number as timeless. Restricting to crashes from $2010$ onward
(Figure~\ref{fig:m0-2010}, $54{,}216$ events) shifts the one-year distribution
markedly relative to the full history: the median rises to roughly break-even
($-0.5\%$) and the mean climbs to $+21\%$, reflecting the sustained post-crisis bull
market over which recoveries were both more frequent and larger. The unconditional
answer to ``is a crash a buying opportunity?'' therefore depends heavily on the
prevailing market regime---a dependence that recurs when we later evaluate predictive
models across time periods. In every era, though, Model~0 remains the benchmark: the
value of a predictive model is whatever it adds on top of these unconditional rates.

\section{Which Features Correlate with Recovery?}

Before building predictive models, we screen individual features for a univariate
association with recovery, again defined as earning money one year after the crash.
These are descriptive relationships---unconditional, uncontrolled for era, sector, or
one another---so they flag where signal may live rather than establish causation. We
begin with company size.

\paragraph{Market capitalization.} We measure size by the point-in-time market
capitalization at the event (the crash-day close times the latest known shares
outstanding), available for $78\%$ of observed events, and split the sample into four
size ranges. Figures~\ref{fig:mcap-micro}--\ref{fig:mcap-mega} show the one-year return
distribution within each range, using the same construction as the Model~0 histograms.
The distribution shifts steadily to the right as size increases: the median one-year
return rises from $-10\%$ for small-caps to $+7\%$ for mega-caps, the mean from $+8\%$
to $+12\%$, and the share of crashes that earn money from $44\%$ to $55\%$. Only among
the largest firms does the median crash actually recover to a profit within a year.

A plausible reading is that large, established firms are more likely to survive and
mean-revert, whereas smaller companies are both more likely to keep sliding and more
likely to be delisted---their distributions are wider on both sides but centered lower.
The effect is real but modest (an $11$-point spread in recovery rate across the size
range) and, being univariate, is confounded with market regime, sector, and price
level. As later sections show, size adds little once price behavior and market context
are modeled; its value here is as a first, interpretable marker that recovery odds are
not uniform across the cross-section.

\paragraph{Crash severity.} We next ask whether the depth of the initial drop predicts
recovery, measuring severity by the crash-day return and splitting events into four
fixed bands from $-10\%$ down to worse than $-30\%$
(Figures~\ref{fig:sev-10}--\ref{fig:sev-30}). A common intuition holds that a larger
drop is a larger overreaction and should therefore rebound more strongly. The data do
not support this for the typical stock: as the crash deepens, the recovery rate
\emph{falls} (from $47\%$ at $10$--$15\%$ to $43\%$ beyond $30\%$) and the median
one-year return worsens monotonically (from $-4\%$ to $-11\%$). Deeper crashes, on
average, keep falling---consistent with a larger drop signaling worse news rather than
a bigger bargain.

The mean, however, tells a subtler story: it stays roughly flat ($+10$ to $+12\%$)
across all severity bands, even as the median deteriorates. The reason is visible in the
histograms: the deepest crashes have the widest distribution, with both a heavier left
mass and a fatter right tail. A severe crash is thus a higher-variance bet---more likely
to keep sliding, but with a larger payoff on the rare occasion it does rebound. Severity
shifts the whole distribution left while stretching its right tail, rather than lifting
recoveries uniformly. As with size, the effect is modest and univariate, and crash
magnitude adds little once price behavior and market context are modeled.

\paragraph{Prior-crash history.} A stock's recent crash history is among the most
informative signals we screen, foreshadowing its prominence in the models. We classify each event by
how many crashes the security suffered in the preceding $20$ trading days---today's
crash never counts as its own prior---into three cohorts: \emph{fresh} (no prior crash;
$59\%$ of events), \emph{one prior} ($22\%$), and \emph{two or more prior} ($19\%$).
Figures~\ref{fig:prior-0}--\ref{fig:prior-2} show the one-year return distribution for
each. The relationship is the strongest we have seen: the median one-year return falls
from $-2\%$ for fresh crashes to $-7\%$ with one prior crash and $-14\%$ with two or
more, while the recovery rate drops from $49\%$ to $43\%$. Repeatedly crashing over a
few weeks is a strong signal of a company in genuine decline, and over a year those
names keep falling.

As with crash severity, the mean moves the opposite way to the median---rising slightly
to $+11\%$ for the repeat-crash cohort even as its median collapses---because the
distribution widens with each additional crash, developing the heaviest left mass and
the fattest right tail of any slice in this section. Repeat crashers are thus the
highest-variance bet: most continue to decline, but a minority rebound spectacularly.
One caution on horizon: this is the one-year picture, and the sign can invert over
shorter windows, where beaten-down, high-volatility names are unusually prone to a
near-term technical bounce; recent-crash history accordingly enters the predictive
models as a powerful but horizon-dependent signal.

\paragraph{Valuation (P/E).} The features above are all derived from price and trading
behavior; valuation is our one fundamental screen. We compute each event's
price-to-earnings ratio at the crash---the crash-day market capitalization over
trailing-twelve-month net income---and group events into unprofitable firms (negative
earnings, hence no meaningful P/E) and three valuation tiers among profitable firms:
cheap ($0<\text{P/E}\le 15$), fair ($15<\text{P/E}\le 30$), and expensive
($\text{P/E}>30$). A P/E is available for $74\%$ of observed events;
Figures~\ref{fig:pe-unprofitable}--\ref{fig:pe-expensive} show the one-year return
distribution for each group.

This is the sharpest relationship in the section. Unprofitable companies fare
disastrously: a median one-year return of $-28\%$, only $35\%$ earning money, and a
\emph{negative} mean---the sole slice in this paper whose average crash loses money over
the following year. Among profitable firms, cheaper is dramatically better: cheap stocks
recover strongly ($61\%$ earn money, median $+16\%$, mean $+31\%$), fair-valued ones
modestly ($52\%$, median $+2\%$), and expensive ones poorly ($42\%$, median $-13\%$).
The spread---from $35\%$ to $61\%$ in recovery rate---is by far the widest we observe. A
crash in a cheap, profitable company looks like an overreaction that tends to reverse; a
crash in an unprofitable or richly-valued one looks like the start of a longer decline.

Two caveats temper this striking result. First, valuation is heavily confounded with
size and quality: unprofitable firms are disproportionately micro-caps, so this screen
partly re-expresses the size effect. Second, and importantly, the association is a
\emph{long-horizon} one. The predictive models developed later target a much shorter
($20$-trading-day) recovery, and over that window fundamentals---valuation
included---add little; value reversion after a crash is a slow process that plays out
over months, not weeks. Valuation thus looks powerful for the one-year question studied
here yet nearly irrelevant for short-term rebound prediction, a horizon distinction we
return to in the modeling results.

\section{A One-Year Recovery Model}
\label{sec:model}

The descriptive screen showed that individual features tilt the one-year odds. A model
asks the sharper question: combined, do these features yield a \emph{calibrated} per-crash
probability of recovery? We keep the Model~0 target of Section~\ref{sec:m0}---the event
earns money if its survivorship-safe one-year total return from the crash-day close is
positive---and estimate $P(\text{earn money in a year})$ with gradient-boosted trees
(XGBoost), which absorb missing fundamentals natively and capture the nonlinear
interactions the histograms only hinted at.

\subsection{Organizing the data: an expanding-window walk-forward}

A single train/test split stakes the whole verdict on one test period, and the descriptive
section showed how much the \emph{vintage} matters---a crash's one-year outcome depends heavily
on the era it falls into. So instead of one split we evaluate the way the model would actually be
deployed. For each year $Y$ from 2005 to 2024 we train only on crashes whose full one-year outcome
precedes $Y$ (a one-year embargo: crash date $\le$ Dec $Y{-}2$), predict every crash in year $Y$,
and set those predictions aside. Concatenating all years yields one out-of-sample set of
$82{,}970$ crashes spanning two decades, each scored by a model that never saw its era. This is
both a more honest evaluation---no single vintage can flatter or damn it---and the real production
loop: retrain as crashes accumulate (the training set grows from $177$k events in 2005 to $255$k
in 2024) and always score the next period with the maximum available history.

The vintage effect is large. The base recovery rate swings from $36\%$ (2022--2024) to $60\%$
(2019--2021, whose crashes were measured through the 2020--21 rebound), and no static model can
anticipate that swing. What is stable is the model's \emph{edge over each vintage's own base
rate}: its top decile beats the contemporaneous base rate in all six three-year blocks
(Table~\ref{tab:vintages}).

\subsection{What the model sees}

The model is given the ten features from the feature-correlation section---log market cap,
crash severity, prior-crash count (20d), P/E, pre-crash one-year return, 252-day beta, EBITDA
margin, revenue growth, market 20-day return, and crash-day return relative to sector. To let
it recognize the \emph{environment} a stock is crashing into---the direct remedy for the
non-stationary base rate above---we optionally add five point-in-time market-regime features,
all trailing (hence knowable at the crash-day close): the market's trailing 126-day and
252-day return, its drawdown from the trailing 52-week high, its trailing 60-day realized
volatility, and \emph{crash breadth} (the fraction of the universe crashing $\ge$$10\%$ in a
day, averaged over the trailing 20 days). Hyperparameters are fixed to the set from a predeclared
grid search; they are not re-tuned inside each walk-forward window, a standard simplification that
keeps every year's model comparable.

\paragraph{The probabilities are well calibrated.} Pooled over all vintages the model's

$8\%$ of the time, because their outcomes ran straight into the 2008 crisis, and 2021's picks
ran into the 2022 bear. Regime features let the model recognize environments it has seen; they
cannot conjure a base rate for one it has not.

\section{Robustness: How Deep a Crash?}
\label{sec:robustness}

Everything so far defines a crash as a single-day drop of at least $10\%$. Does the result survive
a stricter definition? A deeper crash is simply a $-10\%$ event with a more negative crash-day
return, so we subset the events by depth and re-run the entire pooled walk-forward at thresholds of
$-15\%$, $-20\%$, and $-30\%$---same features, same target, same 2005--2024 pooling
(Table~\ref{tab:threshold}). (One caveat: path features such as the prior-crash count stay defined
at the $-10\%$ threshold; only the event set deepens.)

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrrr}
\toprule
Crash & Events & Base & Base & ROC & ECE & Bottom-dec.\ & Top-dec.\ \\
def.\ & & earn & median & AUC & & earn & earn / median \\
\midrule
$-10\%$ & 82{,}970 & 51\% & $+2\%$  & 0.666 & 0.045 & 30\% & 79\% / $+60\%$ \\
$-15\%$ & 25{,}379 & 50\% & $-0\%$  & \textbf{0.700} & 0.037 & 25\% & 86\% / $+96\%$ \\
$-20\%$ &  9{,}967 & 48\% & $-4\%$  & \textbf{0.703} & 0.055 & 22\% & 86\% / $+104\%$ \\
$-30\%$ &  2{,}304 & 43\% & $-13\%$ & 0.660 & 0.071 & 20\% & 75\% / $+77\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Pooled out-of-sample walk-forward at four crash-depth thresholds. As crashes deepen the
\emph{average} outcome worsens (base earn and median fall) while the model's \emph{discrimination}
sharpens (AUC rises, top-decile payoff grows), until sample size collapses at $-30\%$.}
\label{tab:threshold}
\end{table}

The table pulls in two directions at once, and the tension is the point.

\paragraph{On average, deeper is worse.} The base recovery rate falls monotonically from $51\%$ at
$-10\%$ to $43\%$ at $-30\%$, and the base median return drops from $+2\%$ to $-13\%$. A larger
single-day drop carries more genuine damage, not a bigger reflexive bounce: the naive ``buy the
biggest dips'' rule gets \emph{worse} the deeper it is applied.

\paragraph{But the model gets sharper, and the top decile pays far more.} Out-of-sample ROC-AUC
rises from $0.666$ at $-10\%$ to $0.70$ at $-15$ and $-20\%$, and the top-decile median return
roughly doubles, from $+60\%$ to $+104\%$. The bottom-to-top spread widens with it (at $-20\%$,
$22\%$ vs.\ $86\%$ earn). Deeper crashes are a more \emph{bimodal} population---more terminal losers
\emph{and} more violent overreaction-rebounds---so the average sinks while the top-decile payoff
explodes, and the model separates the two best exactly where the stakes are highest. This is the
temporary-overreaction-versus-fundamental-damage distinction the project set out to test, and it is
sharpest precisely where crashes are deepest.

\paragraph{Caveats.} Two. The $-30\%$ row rests on only $2{,}304$ pooled crashes; its lower AUC
($0.66$) and higher ECE ($0.07$) are small-sample noise, not evidence of a real decline in signal.
And as in Section~\ref{sec:model}, the absolute magnitudes are vintage-weighted---inflated by the
2008 and 2020 rebound cohorts---so the trustworthy reading is the \emph{pattern} (deeper crashes are
more separable and their top decile pays more), not the literal $+104\%$. The practical sweet spot
is $-15\%$ to $-20\%$: the strongest discrimination, the largest top-decile payoff, and still enough
events to trust.

\paragraph{How the model performs, by depth.} Figures~\ref{fig:thr-10}--\ref{fig:thr-30} put the
model's \emph{picks} in the Section~\ref{sec:m0} histogram: the one-year return distribution of its
top-decile (highest predicted recovery) crashes, pooled out-of-sample across 2005--2024, at each
crash depth. Following those picks, the distribution shifts hard to the right as the crash deepens.
At $-10\%$ the median pick returns $+60\%$ and $79\%$ earn money; at $-20\%$ the median more than
\emph{doubles} to $+104\%$ and $86\%$ earn. Deeper crashes give the model a more bimodal population
to sort, and its best calls capture the fatter right tail---the picks pile up against the
$\geq{+}200\%$ overflow bin. This is the payoff side of Table~\ref{tab:threshold}, made concrete: the
edge is real and it is larger where crashes are deeper. Two cautions carry over: the levels are
vintage-weighted (the 2008 and 2020 rebound cohorts inflate them, so read the direction, not the
literal $+104\%$), and the $-30\%$ panel rests on only $231$ picks.
